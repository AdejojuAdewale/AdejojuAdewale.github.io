Why I care about data that can’t be reset

You can change a stolen password in a minute. You can’t change a diagnosis. Health data is some of the most sensitive information there is, and once it is exposed, it stays exposed.

I learned that at a primary health care board, where every record I handled belonged to a real patient. One careless moment could cost someone their privacy and their trust in the clinic. It taught me three habits: limit who can see a record, track who touched it, and write procedures other people can follow.

Those are the same habits that make a good security analyst, so I took them into the technical side. I started in labs, then joined Digital Safety Network as an intern and grew into a role on the team, where I monitor SIEM alerts, triage incidents, run vulnerability assessments and build the environments others train in.

Now clinics and hospitals are adopting AI to triage patients, write notes and approve access. That sends sensitive data into systems that can be tricked, leaked or misused. I want to be the person who understands both sides: the data and the intelligence reading it.

Featured · July 2026

Operation Blue Sentinel: a working SOC on a small budget

As Security Engineer in a three-person team, I helped build and run a SOC environment: Wazuh monitoring an Azure-hosted Linux VM and a Windows VM, enriched with VirusTotal threat intelligence. I wrote the documentation, then ran a simulated phishing and brute-force scenario against a fictional retailer to test our response.

804failed SSH logins in one hour from real internet scanners
2custom Wazuh rules for VirusTotal verdicts
35+simulated brute-force attempts
0credentials compromised
  1. Detect: custom alerts fired on the first failed login, and every attempt was timestamped in Wazuh.
  2. Contain: after the incident was declared, I applied a firewall block against the attacking source.
  3. Eradicate: root-cause analysis found an exposed SSH port, which I closed. A follow-up scan found no indicators of compromise.

Lesson: detection worked, but closing unnecessary ports would have prevented the incident entirely. Low-cost, open-source tools are enough to give a small organization real visibility.
